MODULE 1.1Beginner

What Is a Computer Network?

The one idea everything else in this subject is built on.

Real world first

Imagine two houses on a street with a wire strung between them and a bell on each end. Person A writes a note, pulls it across the wire to Person B. That wire + the two houses = the smallest possible network. Add more houses and more wires, and now notes can travel further, through relays.

Definition A computer network is a group of two or more devices connected together so they can share data, resources, and communicate with each other.
  • 01Node — any device on the network: a laptop, phone, printer, or server.
  • 02Link — the connection between two nodes: a cable, a Wi-Fi signal, or fiber.
  • 03Data doesn't travel as one giant file. It's chopped into small pieces called packets, sent separately, and reassembled at the end.
  • 04No network = every computer is an isolated island. No sharing, no internet, no email.
💡Real-world check: Your phone connecting to a Wi-Fi router, that router connecting to your ISP, and your ISP connecting to the rest of the world — that's three networks chained together to form the internet.

MODULE 1.2Beginner

LAN vs MAN vs WAN

Networks classified by how far they reach.

Real world first

LAN is talking to your sibling inside your own house. MAN is shouting across your neighborhood so the next few streets hear you. WAN is mailing a letter to a friend in another country — it needs relays, carriers, and time to arrive.

Definition Network types are classified by geographic scope — how large an area they cover, from a single room to the entire planet.
TypeFull formCoversTypical owner
LANLocal Area NetworkOne building / home / officeYou / your company
MANMetropolitan Area NetworkA city or campusISP / university
WANWide Area NetworkCountry / continent / globeTelecom companies (the internet itself is the biggest WAN)
💡Your home Wi-Fi is a LAN. Your city's cable/fiber ISP backbone is a MAN. The internet connecting you to a server in another country is a WAN.

Same center point, three rings of scale. Watch how the "reach" grows outward — LAN inside your walls, MAN across a city, WAN across the world.

MODULE 1.3Beginner

Network Topologies

The physical shape data has to travel through.

Real world first

Imagine wiring a landline phone system across 5 houses. Do you run one shared line past every house (Bus)? Connect everyone to a central operator (Star)? Loop the wire in a circle (Ring)? Or wire every house directly to every other house (Mesh)? Same 5 houses, four completely different wiring jobs.

Definition A network topology is the arrangement of nodes and links that determines how devices are physically or logically connected to each other.
TopologyOne cable breaks →CostReal use
BusWhole segment can go downCheapestOld Ethernet (rare today)
StarOnly that device dropsMediumHome / office Wi-Fi router setups
RingWhole ring can break (unless dual ring)MediumLegacy token ring, some fiber backbones
MeshBarely affects anything — many pathsMost expensiveInternet backbone, military networks
⚠Star is what almost every home/office uses today — your router is the center, every device connects directly to it.
ANIMATION — PICK A TOPOLOGY

One shared cable. A message travels its full length, and every node hears it — but only the intended one keeps it.

Every node connects only to the center. A message always goes node → hub → node — never directly between two edge nodes.

Nodes form a closed loop. A message hops node-to-node around the ring until it reaches its target.

Every node connects directly to every other node. Fastest and most fault-tolerant — also the most cabling.

MODULE 1.4Beginner

Hub vs Switch vs Router

Three boxes that look similar but do very different jobs.

Real world first

A hub is a person shouting an announcement into a megaphone — everyone in the room hears it, whether it's for them or not. A switch is a receptionist who reads the name on an envelope and walks it directly to that one person's desk. A router is the post office that decides which envelopes leave the building entirely and get sent to a different city.

Definition A hub broadcasts data to every connected device. A switch sends data only to the specific device it's addressed to. A router forwards data between different networks.
DeviceWorks atDecides usingSends to
HubPhysical layerNothing — no intelligenceEveryone (broadcast)
SwitchData Link layerMAC addressOne specific device
RouterNetwork layerIP addressThe correct destination network
💡Hubs are basically extinct now (too wasteful/insecure). Almost everything you plug an Ethernet cable into today is a switch. Your Wi-Fi "router" at home is actually a switch + router + Wi-Fi access point combined into one box.
ANIMATION — PICK A DEVICE

Every line lights up at once — the hub blasts data to all four devices, even though only one (green) actually needs it.

Only one line lights up. The switch checked the destination MAC address and sent data directly to that device only.

The router sits at the boundary between two separate networks and forwards packets across, using IP addresses to decide the path.

MODULE 1.5Beginner

Bandwidth vs Latency

The two numbers people mix up the most when they say "the internet is slow."

Real world first

Think of a highway. Bandwidth is how many lanes it has — more lanes, more cars per minute get through. Latency is how long it takes one single car to drive from start to finish, no matter how many lanes exist. A highway can have 20 lanes (huge bandwidth) but still take an hour to cross (high latency) if it's very long.

Definition Bandwidth is the maximum amount of data that can be transferred per second. Latency is the time delay for one piece of data to travel from source to destination.
  • 01Bandwidth is measured in bits per second — Mbps, Gbps.
  • 02Latency is measured in milliseconds (ms) — the "ping" number you see in games.
  • 03High bandwidth + high latency = a video call that's crisp quality but has an annoying delay (common on satellite internet).
  • 04Low bandwidth + low latency = instant response but things load slowly (old dial-up era, sort of).
⚠"My internet is slow" could mean either problem, and the fix is different for each. Streaming a movie cares about bandwidth. Online gaming and video calls care more about latency.
ANIMATION — TWO DIFFERENT KINDS OF "SLOW"

Top strip: several packets moving together — that's raw capacity. Bottom strip: one packet's full journey time — that's delay. They're independent numbers.

MODULE 2.1Intermediate

Why Layers Exist (Encapsulation)

How one message becomes a stack of nested envelopes before it ever leaves your computer.

Real world first

You're shipping a gift internationally. You put the gift in a box (your data). You add a shipping label with the receiver's address (like a network address). The courier adds their own tracking sticker and route code (like a physical transport tag). Each department adds their wrapper without caring what's inside the previous one — they just do their one job and pass it on.

Definition Encapsulation is the process of wrapping data with a new header (and sometimes a trailer) at each layer as it travels down the network stack before being sent over the wire.
  • 01Each layer only understands its own header — it never opens the layers wrapped inside it.
  • 02At the destination, the process runs in reverse: decapsulation — each layer strips off its own header until the raw data is left.
  • 03This is why layers exist at all: it lets each layer be built, fixed, or upgraded independently. Wi-Fi can be swapped for fiber without rewriting how browsers work.
💡By the time your one email leaves your laptop, it's wrapped in roughly 4 layers of headers — and every router along the way reads only the outer ones it needs, just like a courier reads the shipping label, not the gift receipt inside.
ANIMATION — DATA GETTING WRAPPED

Watch each header wrap around the previous layer — data → transport → network → data link — right before it leaves as electrical/light/radio signal.

MODULE 2.2Intermediate

The 7 OSI Layers

Click any layer in the diagram — it's the standard map everyone in networking uses to talk about "where" a problem is.

Real world first

Think of a company shipping a package through 7 departments: Loading Dock → Local Courier → Routing Center → Delivery Confirmation → Session Desk → Translation Desk → Customer Front Desk. Each department has exactly one job and hands off to the next. The OSI model is that same idea applied to network communication.

Definition The OSI (Open Systems Interconnection) Model is a 7-layer conceptual framework that standardizes how different networking protocols communicate with each other.
💡Memory trick (bottom to top): Please Do Not Throw Sausage Pizza Away → Physical, Data Link, Network, Transport, Session, Presentation, Application.

MODULE 2.3Intermediate

TCP/IP 4-Layer Model

OSI is the theory. TCP/IP is what the real internet actually runs on.

Real world first

OSI's 7 departments look great on a org chart, but in practice, some neighboring departments always work so closely together that people just think of them as one team. TCP/IP is what happens when you merge OSI's 7 official departments into the 4 teams that actually exist in the real building.

Definition The TCP/IP model condenses OSI's 7 layers into 4 practical layers, and it's the actual model the internet is built on.
TCP/IP LayerMatches OSI LayersHandles
ApplicationApp + Presentation + Session (5,6,7)HTTP, DNS, FTP, email
TransportTransport (4)TCP, UDP — ports, reliability
InternetNetwork (3)IP addressing, routing
Network AccessData Link + Physical (1,2)Ethernet, Wi-Fi, MAC, cabling
💡When engineers say "Layer 3 problem" or "Layer 7 firewall," they're using OSI numbering even though the internet runs on TCP/IP — the numbering just stuck around as universal shorthand.
